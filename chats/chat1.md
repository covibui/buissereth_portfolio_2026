## The core repositioning problem

The hardest part of going from practitioner to advisor isn't visual — it's **what you take credit for**. Designers narrate *decisions* ("I redesigned the checkout flow, used a card layout, ran usability tests"). Consultants narrate *judgment* ("the business was losing high-intent users at the worst possible moment; I reframed checkout as a trust problem, not a UI problem"). Same project, completely different altitude. Your whole portfolio has to operate one level up from where a design portfolio sits.

So the central thesis can't be "I make good products." It has to be a **claim about how value gets created** that you happen to prove through design work. Something like: *"Most companies treat design as the last mile of execution. I treat it as the place strategy gets tested against reality — and that's where the real leverage is."* That's a thesis a CEO nods at. We need to find yours.

## 1. Positioning & narrative

Push #1: **Don't lead with your design background. Lead with the problem you solve.** The moment your hero says "UX designer with 10 years…" you've capped your rate and your altitude. Buyers categorize you in the first 5 seconds. Advisors lead with a worldview or a problem; practitioners lead with a discipline.

Push #2: **"Strategy" is the most diluted word in this market.** Every designer now claims it. You differentiate by being *specific and opinionated*, not by claiming the label. A sharp thesis + a named methodology beats "I do design strategy" every time.

## 2. Content & case selection

What business buyers actually want to see:
- **Before/after at the business level** — not "old screen → new screen" but "the company believed X; we discovered Y; the decision changed; here's what it was worth."
- **Evidence of judgment under ambiguity** — a moment you *killed* a feature, reframed a brief, or told a client the thing they didn't want to hear. That single anecdote signals "advisor" louder than any metric.
- **Outcomes in the buyer's language** — revenue, retention, cycle time, risk avoided, decisions accelerated. "Increased engagement 12%" is a designer metric. "Cut a six-month roadmap debate to a two-week decision" is a consultant metric.

Push #3: **Fewer cases, deeper. Three is more authoritative than nine.** A thought-leader platform implies selectivity. Nine cases says "hire me, I'm available." Three says "these are the kinds of problems I take."

## 3. Editorial structure & tone

An editorial portfolio reads like a *publication with a point of view*, not a gallery. In practice that means:
- **The writing carries the weight, images support it** — inverse of a normal design portfolio.
- **A strong masthead / thesis statement** up top, like a magazine's editorial stance.
- **Cases framed as essays or arguments**, with a headline that makes a *claim*, not a label ("How a fintech stopped shipping features nobody asked for" vs. "Acme Bank — Mobile Redesign").
- **Pull quotes, considered typography, generous whitespace, restraint.** Authority comes from what you leave out.
- Voice: **declarative, first-person, opinionated, calm.** Consultants make assertions. Designers describe processes. "I believe X" / "Most teams get this wrong" reads advisor. "I collaborated with stakeholders to iterate on…" reads vendor.

## 4. Audience & gaps

Who's the buyer matters enormously and changes everything downstream — which is my first question. A founder/CEO buys differently than a VP of Product, who buys differently than a design leader hiring a consultant, who buys differently than a procurement-driven enterprise. The *evidence* that builds confidence differs for each.

What it must say: a clear thesis, proof of business judgment, selectivity, and a sense that you operate as a peer to executives.
What it must **not** say: rates, availability, "open to work," tool lists, process diagrams as hero content, or anything that frames you as a pair of hands.
